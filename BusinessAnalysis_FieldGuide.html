The Business Analyst Field Guide

The ~20% of business analysis an AI Solution Architect must master — not to write requirements by hand, but to direct, evaluate, and approve what AI-generated requirements produce. Every technique here is demonstrated against one live example: ClaimSwift AI, an AI-powered claims intake initiative at Meridian Mutual Insurance.

Why Business Analysis Exists — and Why It's Now Your Job to Judge It

Business analysis exists to close the gap between what the business needs and what gets built. Every failed software project traces back to the same root cause more often than any other: requirements that were wrong, ambiguous, untestable, or simply never validated with the people who would live with the consequences. A BA's job is to prevent that — by eliciting real needs, structuring them so nothing is lost in translation, and proving traceability from business goal to shipped feature.

As an AI Solution Architect, you will rarely write a user story from a blank page. AI drafting tools will generate BRDs, stories, and acceptance criteria for you in seconds. Your value is no longer in the typing — it's in the judgment: can you tell a good AI-generated requirement from a dangerous one before it reaches a sprint board? This guide teaches the ~20% of BA craft that makes that judgment possible.

The architect's mandate You are not the BA. You direct what the AI elicits, evaluate what it drafts against the standards on this page, and approve (or send back) before anything becomes a commitment to stakeholders, engineers, or regulators.

The 20% That Covers 80% of the Job

Functional vs. Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements (FR) describe what the system does — a behavior, a rule, an output. Non-functional requirements (NFR) describe how well it does it — performance, security, availability, compliance, usability. AI drafts routinely produce strong FRs and weak, vague, or missing NFRs. That gap is exactly where architects earn their keep: an AI-triage engine that classifies claims correctly (FR) but takes 40 seconds per request (missing NFR) will fail in production even though every functional test passes.

Traceability

Every requirement should trace back to a business goal and forward to a test case. If you cannot answer "why does this requirement exist?" and "how will we prove it's done?", the requirement is an orphan — and orphans are how scope creep and untestable features get through review. See the Requirements Traceability Matrix for how this is enforced end to end.

User Stories & Acceptance Criteria

The standard shape is As a <persona>, I want <capability>, so that <benefit>. A story without a benefit clause is a task in disguise — it tells engineering what to build but gives nobody a way to judge whether it was worth building. Acceptance criteria written as Given / When / Then turn a story into something testable: a precondition, a trigger, an observable outcome. If you can't write the Given/When/Then, the story isn't ready for a sprint.

MoSCoW Prioritization

Must have — the release fails without it. Should have — important, painful to cut, but not fatal. Could have — desirable if time allows. Won't have (this time) — explicitly out of scope, with a reason, so it doesn't quietly resurface as scope creep. A backlog where everything is "Must" has not been prioritized at all — it's a red flag that the analysis was rubber-stamped, not reasoned through.

Personas & Use Cases

Personas capture goals and frustrations, not demographics — a persona exists to keep every requirement anchored to a real person's motivation, not an abstract "the user." Use cases describe the full interaction between an actor and the system: not just the happy path, but the alternate paths (valid variations) and exception / unhappy paths (what happens when something goes wrong). AI-generated use cases frequently document only the happy path — that omission is the single most common gap you will be asked to catch.

KPIs

Key Performance Indicators turn "this will improve things" into a number someone can be held accountable for. Good KPIs are specific, have a baseline, a target, and a measurement cadence. "Improve customer satisfaction" is not a KPI. "Raise first-contact NPS from 21 to 45 within 12 months, measured monthly via post-interaction survey" is.

The Architect's Review Lens

Use this checklist every time an AI tool (or a human analyst) hands you a requirement, story, or document for approval:

  • Traceable — Does it link to a business goal and a KPI? Would removing it measurably hurt an outcome anyone cares about?
  • Testable — Can QA write a pass/fail test from this sentence alone, with no further clarification?
  • Singular — Is it one requirement, not three joined by "and"?
  • Prioritized honestly — Is the MoSCoW label defensible, or is everything "Must" because nobody wants to have the prioritization conversation?
  • Complete on NFRs — Performance, security, compliance, availability, usability — addressed or explicitly deferred with a reason?
  • Covers the unhappy path — Does the use case or story account for what happens when data is missing, a system is down, or a human disagrees with the AI?
  • Bounded — Is scope explicit about what's excluded, not just what's included?
  • Owned — Is there a named business owner who can accept the requirement and be accountable for the KPI it drives?
Common AI-drafting failure modes to watch for Vague quantifiers ("fast", "user-friendly", "robust") standing in for numbers; every story marked Must; acceptance criteria that restate the story instead of testing it; use cases with no alternate or exception flow; NFRs missing entirely; personas that are demographic sketches instead of goal-and-frustration profiles.

The rest of this guide demonstrates every one of these techniques against a single running example: ClaimSwift AI, a first-notice-of-loss automation initiative at a fictional regional insurer, Meridian Mutual Insurance. The Ask

Approve $2.4M in Year-0 capital and $480K in annual run-rate funding to deploy ClaimSwift AI: an AI-powered conversational intake, severity triage, and fraud-signal engine covering Auto and Homeowners First Notice of Loss (FNOL) claims across all six Meridian Mutual regional claims centers.

The Problem

FNOL intake at Meridian Mutual is manual, slow, and inconsistent. Callers wait an average of 22.4 minutes to report a loss; claims then sit 4.3 days in an unassigned queue before a human triages severity by hand. Only 6% of claims move through without manual intervention. The result: $8.2M in annual leakage from severity misclassification, a first-contact NPS of 21, and a Special Investigations Unit (SIU) drowning in low-precision fraud referrals (34% precision).

The Solution

ClaimSwift AI replaces manual FNOL intake with a conversational AI agent (voice and web chat) that captures structured loss data in one conversation, auto-classifies claim complexity and severity, auto-assigns low-complexity claims directly to available adjusters, and generates an explainable fraud-signal score for every new claim. Human adjusters remain the final authority on every decision — the AI recommends, adjusters approve or override.

Scope & Guardrails

1. Vision Statement

Vision "Every Meridian Mutual policyholder reporting a loss is heard immediately, understood accurately, and connected to the right adjuster within hours — not days — while every claim decision remains explainable, auditable, and ultimately owned by a licensed human adjuster."

2. Business Problem

Meridian Mutual (460,000 active policies; Auto, Homeowners, Renters; 6 regional claims centers; ~$390M paid claims annually) runs FNOL intake as a largely manual, phone-first process built on a 2016-era Guidewire ClaimCenter implementation. As claim volume has grown 18% over three years without proportional headcount growth, four symptoms have become structural risks:

  • Average FNOL call handle time of 22.4 minutes versus an industry benchmark near 12 minutes.
  • Claims wait an average of 4.3 days for an adjuster assignment, during which evidence quality (photos, witness statements) degrades.
  • Severity misclassification at intake drives an estimated $8.2M/year in claim leakage (2.1% of paid claims).
  • The SIU fraud-referral process is manual and imprecise (34% precision), burning investigator time on low-signal cases while high-signal cases go unflagged.

3. Strategic Alignment

This initiative directly supports two of Meridian Mutual's FY2026–2028 strategic pillars: "Claims Experience Modernization" (CEO strategic priority #2) and "Loss Ratio Discipline" (CFO strategic priority #1). It is sponsored by the Chief Claims Officer and co-funded by the Claims Operations and Finance organizations.

Business Requirements Document

ClaimSwift AI — FNOL Intake, Triage & Fraud Signal

1. Purpose & Scope

This BRD defines the business requirements for ClaimSwift AI Phase 1: an AI-powered conversational intake, severity/complexity triage, auto-assignment, and fraud-signal engine for Auto and Homeowners First Notice of Loss (FNOL) claims. It is the requirements baseline against which the User Stories, Use Cases, and Requirements Traceability Matrix are built.

Out of scope — Phase 1 Commercial Lines, Umbrella, and Specialty policies; automated settlement authority of any dollar amount (see US-15, deferred pending legal review of delegated settlement authority); claims already assigned to an adjuster prior to go-live (no retroactive re-triage).

2. Business Objectives

  • Reduce average FNOL intake time from 22.4 to 9.0 minutes within 12 months of full rollout.
  • Raise straight-through processing (STP) rate for low-complexity claims from 6% to 35%.
  • Reduce annual claim leakage attributable to severity misclassification by $1.1M in Year 1.
  • Improve SIU fraud-referral precision from 34% to 55%.
  • Raise first-contact NPS from 21 to 45.

5. Assumptions, Constraints & Dependencies

  • Assumption: Guidewire ClaimCenter and PolicyCenter expose stable, documented APIs for real-time claim creation and coverage verification.
  • Constraint: No automated final coverage or settlement decision may be issued without licensed-adjuster review, per state adjuster-licensing statute.
  • Dependency: SMS evidence upload (FR-03) depends on the existing Twilio contract held by the Marketing organization; a data-sharing agreement is required before build.
  • Constraint: Spanish-language intake (NFR-10) requires a certified insurance-domain translation review before go-live, not just machine translation.

Main Flow

  3. AI verifies the policy against PolicyCenter in real time.
  4. AI asks the policyholder to describe what happened, in their own words.
  5. AI extracts loss type, date/time, and location from the conversation, confirming ambiguous details.
  6. AI confirms applicable coverage and deductible in plain language.
  7. AI creates a structured claim record in ClaimCenter and provides the policyholder a claim number.
  8. AI sends an SMS evidence-upload link (FR-03) and closes the session.
Alternate Flow A1 — Escalation to Human Agent
At any point, the policyholder may say "talk to a person." The AI summarizes captured details, transfers the session (with context) to the next available human intake agent, and logs the escalation reason.
Alternate Flow A2 — Multiple Losses in One Call
If the policyholder describes two unrelated losses (e.g., a windshield claim and a separate water-damage claim), the AI opens two independent claim records and confirms both claim numbers before ending the session.
Exception Flow E1 — Policy Not Found or Lapsed
If PolicyCenter returns no active match, the AI does not fabricate a claim record. It informs the policyholder a specialist will follow up within one business hour, logs a manual-review ticket, and ends the session without creating a claim.
Exception Flow E2 — Low-Confidence Extraction
If the AI cannot extract loss type or location with sufficient confidence after two clarifying questions, it flags the claim "manual review required" and routes to a human intake agent rather than guessing.

Main Flow
  1. Fraud-signal model scores the claim 0–100 using claim details, policy tenure, and historical patterns.
  2. If the score exceeds the referral threshold (70), the claim is auto-referred to the SIU queue.
  3. System generates a plain-language explainability report listing contributing indicators.
  4. SIU analyst reviews the referral and opens or declines a formal investigation.
  5. Analyst records the outcome (confirmed fraud / false positive) against the referral.
Alternate Flow A1 — Analyst Disputes Score
If the analyst believes the score is unwarranted, they record "false positive" immediately with a note; the case is excluded from active investigation but retained for quarterly model retraining (US-10).
Exception Flow E1 — Insufficient Evidence Data
If key inputs (e.g., prior claim history) are unavailable due to a data outage, the model does not force a score. The claim is flagged "fraud score pending" and re-scored automatically once data is available.

Current State — Manual FNOL Intake

  1. Policyholder calls the contact center to report a loss.
  2. IVR routes the call to an available agent (or the after-hours outsourced partner, Alorica).
  3. Agent manually keys loss details into ClaimCenter while the policyholder is on the line.
  4. Claim sits in an unassigned queue — average wait 4.3 days.
  5. Claims Manager manually reviews the queue each morning and assigns claims by rough region and line of business.
  6. Adjuster reviews the claim and chases missing documents by phone and email.
  7. Adjuster manually estimates severity and sets a reserve.
  8. Adjuster manually screens for fraud indicators and refers suspicious claims to SIU by email.
  9. SIU manually investigates every referral, regardless of signal strength (34% precision).
  10. Adjuster negotiates settlement and closes the claim.

Future State — ClaimSwift AI-Assisted Intake

  1. Policyholder engages the AI voice or chat intake agent, available 24/7.
  2. AI auto-extracts structured loss data and verifies policy and coverage in real time.
  3. AI classifies severity/complexity and generates a fraud-signal score.
  4. Low-complexity claims are instantly auto-assigned via the load-balancing rules engine.
  5. Medium/High-complexity claims route to a senior adjuster queue with an AI-prefilled worksheet.
  6. Adjuster validates the AI classification — accepts or overrides with a reason code.
  7. High fraud-signal claims are auto-referred to SIU with a full explainability report.
  8. Adjuster settles the claim; policyholder receives proactive SMS milestone updates.
  9. Compliance dashboard monitors AI decisions monthly for audit-readiness and fairness.
Net process impact Steps 4–6 of the current state (unassigned queue, manual review, document chasing) collapse into a single automated classification-and-assignment step for the 35% of claims classified Low complexity — the single largest driver of the 4.3-day-to-4-hour improvement in time-to-assignment.

FAQ & Glossary

Quick answers to the questions learners ask most often. For a conversational version, use the Ask the Guide widget above.

What's the difference between a functional and a non-functional requirement?

A functional requirement describes a behavior the system must perform (e.g., "auto-classify claim complexity"). A non-functional requirement describes the quality bar that behavior must meet (e.g., "classify within 60 seconds, with 88% accuracy, encrypted at rest"). See the BRD for 14 functional and 10 non-functional examples side by side.

What does MoSCoW mean and how strict should it be?

Must / Should / Could / Won't. Must means the release fails without it; Won't is explicitly excluded (with a reason) so it doesn't quietly resurface as scope creep later. In the User Stories backlog, 7 of 15 stories are Must — if every story were Must, that would be a signal the prioritization conversation never happened.

Why do use cases need alternate and exception flows?

The main flow is the happy path. Alternate flows are valid variations (e.g., escalating to a human mid-conversation). Exception flows cover failure (e.g., the policy isn't found, or the AI can't classify with confidence). AI-generated use cases most often skip exception flows entirely — see UC-1 through UC-4 for what "complete" looks like.

What makes a requirement traceable, and why does it matter?

Traceable means it links backward to a business objective and forward to a verification method. The Requirements Traceability Matrix is the artifact that proves this for every requirement in the BRD — no row has a dash in both the objective and verification columns.

What is a persona, and how is it different from a user role?

A user role is a job title. A persona is a specific person's goals and frustrations — it exists to keep requirements anchored to a real motivation instead of an abstract "the user." See the five Personas built for ClaimSwift AI, none of which lead with demographic details.

As an AI Solution Architect, what should I actually be checking before I approve an AI-generated requirement?

Run the eight-point Architect's Review Lens: traceable, testable, singular, honestly prioritized, complete on NFRs, covers the unhappy path, bounded in scope, and owned by a named business stakeholder.

What is straight-through processing (STP) and why does ClaimSwift AI care about it?

STP is the percentage of claims that move from intake to adjuster assignment with zero manual triage. Meridian Mutual's baseline is 6%; ClaimSwift AI targets 35% for low-complexity Auto and Homeowners claims. See the Vision & Business Case for the full current-state / future-state comparison.

Why does the AI never make a final settlement decision on its own?

State adjuster-licensing statute requires a licensed human to own final coverage and settlement decisions. This is why US-15 (AI auto-negotiating settlements under $2,500) is explicitly marked Won't for this release — it's deferred pending legal review, not silently dropped.
